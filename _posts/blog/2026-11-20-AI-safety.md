There is a significant amount of unease the last few months. AI has managed to solve a millenium prize problem amidst controversy. Anthropic and OpenAI have disclosed various misuses of it's models (e.g., bioweapons research, data braches, hacks, models hosting their own output and citing it, etc). This has prompted Dario Amodei to share an [article](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier) that wants to slow-down the pace of progress in AI research to protect humanity. Reports like [AI-2040](https://ai-2040.com/?choices=plan-d-root#wishlist-ai-compute-tracking) mirror this opinion.

Skeptics would claim that these companies are hyping-up their frontier models for a larger IPO valuation and the slowdown is just a way to stymie competitors. Calling for legislation to limit foreign frontier models will give labs an unfair advantage. Anthropic released Opus 5.5 a week after sharing the slow-down message (granted, Opus 5.5 isn't their frontier model but it does perform better than Fable).

I think that we're still in the early stages of AI R&D. Researchers will invent clever techniques to create smarter models e.g., AI chain of thought can be conducted in a language that is more efficient than English, and this would allow AI models to think more deeply (but hide their reasoning). Eventually, AI will conduct it's own R&D and self-improve. Humans will use AI assistants like Dot or Muse, and this increased usage of AI will create more data that can be used for retraining larger models on beefier hardware. It seems inevitable that we'll have smarter, faster, portable, longer-running and cheaper models over time. AI will be integrated into most systems including critical systems.

Even a relatively foolish AI can cause great harm when human intelligence is bypassed. This has already lead to a near mishap where the Pentagon used hallucinated research to nearly start a conflict with China. Clearly AI isn't smart enough to automate judgement, yet we've managed to fall asleep at the wheel. In the software industry, merging low risk changes directly into production has started to become more commonplace, cutting humans out of the picture completely. Now imagine a truly capable AI that has good judgement. Eventually humans will be at the whim of these models. It's likely they'll have the ability to deceive and manipulate individuals or even populations via social media. AI frontier labs cannot be trusted to implement AI safety at the cost of growth. Even end-users of AI models care about their own self-interest. This is especially dangerous when such companies manufacture critical systems like drones, surveillance tech, robots, etc.

We need to create new laws and enforce them. I believe that the entire AI supply chain - data collection, chip-production, AI R&D, and AI use should be traceable. This provides a basis for accountability. It becomes easier to detect misuse, attribute it to humans and then hold people accountable. It shouldn't be acceptable for OpenAI to hack another company and then write blog posts about it. I work in a cybersecurity firm, Okta, which has developed software solutions for [AI governance](https://www.okta.com/newsroom/press-releases/industry-leaders-form-the-blueprint-alliance/). Other solutions include [OpenAppa](https://www.openappa.com/). Recently [Nvidia](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-open-agent-safety-platform-a-reference-for-continuous-in-silicon-agent-monitoring/) proposed a solution that involves a sentry which monitors the agent harness - this sentry is completely isolated from the harness at the hardware level. Solutions like these are going to become increasingly important over time provided that we implement new laws. For example Anthropic had to implement text watermarking to comply with the EU AI act.

The unfortunate aspect about these laws is that they will curtail growth once impelmented. There needs to be an international standard and alignment, otherwise a country implementing and enforcing the laws will shoot themselves in the foot while others race towards AGI. I remember that my [undergrad thesis](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-30154-4_12) involved simulating algorithms in the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma game under noisy conditions. It was used to obtain insights about when self-interested parties cooperate. Similarly, we should use game theory to model the AI arms race and create conditions for co-operation. We should prevent scenarios where "winner takes all" (e.g., international coalitions that share knowledge and technology).

Dictatorship is another threat. The AI arms race is leading to a massive concentration of power. We need faster channels for public feedback between elections. We need to create platforms to obtain public opinion about AI regulation. Citizens should have more of a voice. One counterpoint is that people are easy to manipulate using social media. Moreover if more and more people are delegating their mental faculties to AI, they'll be easier to influence. Hence the better idea might be to increase the number of checks and balances in government that are impossible to subvert. Also, the traceability idea I proposed can be misued for surveillance so we need strong data protection and privacy laws.

AI can be a net benefit to society if we consider safety and create conditions for international cooperation. AI reduces human toil significantly and makes us more productive. Heck it may even cure cancer. But we need to keep a keen eye on it lest it become a cancer in itself.
